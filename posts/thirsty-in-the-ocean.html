Thirsty in the Ocean

September 14, 2026 | Calculating...

I live in a hostel, far from home and far from the familiar comfort of the people who know me. Here, I live alone. There is no one to talk to, no one to sit with and share the quiet weight of the phase I am walking through.

Even though the corridors outside are filled with people, there is not a single soul to whom I can simply open my heart. Being an introvert, breaking the silence doesn’t come easily to me. On the rare occasions I do gather the courage to reach out, the conversations are usually cut short with a polite, “I’m busy right now.” But I don’t hold it against them. Life has a way of keeping everyone occupied, and everyone is carrying their own responsibilities. I am not complaining that people don’t talk to me. Deep down, though, a quiet whisper sometimes asks: why me?

My classmates live on the very same floor. Every morning, I hear them knocking on each other’s doors, calling out names, and heading to class together in laughter. No one ever knocks on my door. They walk to the mess together; I walk alone. In the evenings, they gather in a single room, their voices spilling out into the hallway. They don’t invite me. And on the rare occasion I stop by for an academic doubt, the laughter pauses into sudden silence. Nobody asks me to sit. I understand the unspoken cue, offer a quiet smile, and retreat back to my own room.

My days have settled into an unbroken rhythm of solitude. Wake up alone. Walk to breakfast alone. Walk to class alone, sit alone on the bench, eat lunch and dinner alone, and return to an empty room at night. When the academic work is done, I lie down, staring at the ceiling, listening to melancholic songs, or watching romantic films. I love stories about life and love because they carry genuine emotion, something I rarely get to touch during the day.

“It feels like standing as a thirsty traveler in the middle of a vast ocean. Water stretches as far as the eye can see, yet there isn’t a single drop to drink.”

Sometimes, when the walls of the room feel too narrow, I walk down to the ghats late at night. I sit by the water until the early hours, watching the dark river flow quietly beneath the night sky. In that stillness, I talk to myself about the past, the present, and whatever the future might hold. Other times, I go to the gym, lifting weights just to let physical exhaustion quiet the restlessness inside.

The heaviest part of this solitude isn’t just being alone; it is the vulnerability of it. If something were to happen to me here, if I were to fall ill, there is no one who would notice or step forward to take care of me. Even the classmates who share my lectures rarely say a word. It puzzles me, because whenever they needed something, whether it was helping with an assignment or accompanying them somewhere outside, even leaving my own work pending, I always said yes. I said yes because I genuinely believed that if you have the ability to help someone, you should. Yet whenever I asked for a small hand, the answer was always that they were too busy. I never argued. I just found a way to figure things out on my own.

Even now, as I write these words, my classmates are down by the ghats together, celebrating the night. And truly, I am happy for them. At least they are laughing; at least they are living life. I don’t hold bitterness toward anyone. Everyone has the right to live as they choose, and everyone is justified in their own way. I only hope that everyone finds happiness, and that no one ever has to live through the kind of quiet loneliness I have known.

I have already walked through heavier storms. During my B.Tech years, my body fought through severe illnesses: tuberculosis, COVID, jaundice, and simultaneous kidney and liver infections. Growing up, I faced my share of discrimination, too. Yet I never held a grudge, because seeing people happy around me was enough. I have never demanded much from life, and even today, I ask for very little. I don’t want anything for myself. My only true dream, the one thing that keeps me moving forward, is to give my parents the comfortable, dignified life they so deeply deserve.

I didn’t write this to complain or seek sympathy. I wrote this simply to pour out what has been sitting heavy inside my chest, so that I can breathe, clear my mind, and get back to the work I need to do.

Late at night, I talk to myself and wonder if there is something flawed in me, or if my kismat was simply written this way. But perhaps it doesn’t matter. I am learning to go with the flow. The feeling is best described like standing as a thirsty traveler in the middle of a vast ocean: people everywhere, yet words remain unreachable. My introversion runs so deep that even when a stranger asks me for directions on the street, words get caught in my throat; my speech stumbles with hesitation.

And that is all. There isn’t much left to say. I hope that everyone finds what their heart is searching for, and that everyone gets to live happily.

- Sumit Sengar
